How custom milling works

Log to lumber, start to finish

The most common reason a landowner doesn't call a sawmill is that they have no idea what the process involves or how long it takes. Here it is, plainly. Timelines shown are typical and confirmed case by case.

Tell us what you have

Species if you know it, rough diameter, length, and how many. A photo of the butt end tells us most of what we need. If you don't know what it is, send the picture — identifying Catskill hardwood is the easy part.

Same week

Getting the log to the mill

Some people bring logs on a trailer. Some need help. We'll tell you honestly whether the log is worth the trip — a log full of fence wire or rot is not, and you should hear that before you pay for a haul.

By arrangement

Sawing to your plan

Thickness, quartersawn or flat, live edge kept or trimmed. What you're building determines how the log gets opened, and that decision can't be undone — so we make it with you, not for you.

Scheduled

Air drying, then the kiln

Freshly sawn boards are stickered and air dried, then finished in the dry kiln to a stable interior moisture content. Skipping this is why homemade slabs cup and split two years after they're built into something.

Months, not days

Dimension milling & planing

Dried stock can be planed and cut to dimension parts, ready to hand to a cabinetmaker or work in your own shop.

Catskill species

What your tree will actually behave like

Gary spent years on local radio talking through one Catskill species at a time — black walnut, black cherry, black locust, tulip poplar, white cedar, aspen. That series is a decade of knowledge no competitor can copy, and it belongs on this website as the reason a landowner calls here first.

Black Walnut

The one everybody hopes they have. Dark heartwood, pale sap, and a value gap between "milled properly" and "cut for firewood" that is measured in thousands.

Black Cherry

Comes off the saw pale and darkens for years afterward. The piece you build will not be the colour it is the day you finish it.

Black Locust

Rot resistant to the point of stubbornness. Wasted indoors — this is outdoor wood: posts, sills, decking that outlives the person who laid it.

Tulip Poplar

Big, straight, light and stable. Underrated for painted work and secondary parts, and there is a great deal of it in these hills.

White Cedar

Light, aromatic, weather tolerant. Traditional for anything that lives outside or stores clothing.

Aspen & Poplar

Soft, pale, quick growing. Not a trophy log — but it has honest uses, and knowing which is which saves everyone a wasted afternoon.

Species notes above are drawn from the themes of Gary's radio series; final wording to be written with him so it carries his own voice rather than a paraphrase.

The dry kiln

The step that decides whether your table survives its first winter

A board straight off a sawmill is full of water. Build with it and it will move — cupping, checking, pulling joints apart in a heated house. Drying is not an upsell; it is the difference between lumber and firewood with square edges.

Air drying gets the wood most of the way. The kiln takes it the rest, to a moisture content that will hold steady in a Catskills house that runs a woodstove all January.

What to expect

Air drying first
Stickered and stacked with airflow — roughly a year per inch of thickness as a rule of thumb.
Then the kiln
Brings interior moisture down to a stable level for indoor furniture and finishes the job properly.
Why not skip it
Wet stock built into furniture is the single most common reason a slab table fails.
Storage
Stock can wait here while it dries — you don't need a barn to start the process.
Cost
Quoted by board foot and species after we see the logs. Published rates to be confirmed with Gary.

About

Gary Mead

Raised on a dairy farm in New Kingston, a few miles from where the mill stands. Left farming in 1979 for a woodworking shop, and never left the valley. Everything on this site — the saw, the dimension mill, the kiln, the gallery — was added one piece at a time over forty-seven years.

Raised in New Kingston

A Delaware County dairy farm, in the heart of the Catskills. Farming didn't take. The woods did.
