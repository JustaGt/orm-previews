Why people drive to Newfield for locust

Most yards will sell you a treated pine post and tell you it lasts twenty years. Black locust is a different argument entirely, and it's worth understanding before you buy.

Rot resistance is in the wood

Black locust is naturally decay-resistant heartwood. Nothing is injected into it, so nothing leaches out of it — which matters when the post is holding up a vegetable bed, a paddock rail or a dock.

No chemicals near the soil

Growers, market gardeners and organic operations use locust specifically because it doesn't put treatment chemicals in contact with the ground their food comes out of.

Hard enough to argue with

It's one of the hardest, densest woods that grows in the Northeast. Drill it, pre-bore it, and it will hold a fastener for decades.

It grew twelve miles from you

An Ithaca architecture studio told us they use our locust in residential builds instead of importing tropical hardwood. That's the whole point of the yard.

Sixty years of one tree

Tom Brown sold his first black locust fence posts as a teenager, more than sixty years ago. He spent his working life as physical plant director at Cornell and Ithaca College, and spent the rest of it on locust — enough that he was known nationally for it and worked on developing his own strains of the tree.

Locust Lumber Company grew out of that. Tom passed in 2019. The yard on Millard Hill Road is still cutting, still selling posts, and still run on the same conviction he had: that the best rot-resistant wood in the Northeast is the one already growing here.
