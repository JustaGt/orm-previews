Why Silver

Chrome flashes. Silver glows — and glows deeper.

Mass-produced spoons wear chrome or nickel. Sutton Spoons are plated in genuine silver because of how it returns light in deep, low-light water — the reason trollers chasing lake trout, browns, and landlocked salmon tie one on when nothing else is moving.

Each spoon is still blanked, formed, and polished with methods the shop has used since 1944 — some of the tools still carry toolmaker George Wells' name, and he learned the trade from Sutton himself around 1875.

The Lineage

Six sets of hands. One way of making a spoon.

1867 — S.R. Sutton, Jeweler

Scott R. Sutton opens his jewelry shop in Naples and starts silver-plating the spoons he fishes on Canandaigua Lake. Other anglers demand them; a tackle line is born.

1915 — George Wells carries it on

Sutton's toolmaker of forty years keeps the spoons in production; many of his hand tools are still on the bench today.

1930 — The Staffords take the wheel

Four fishermen buy the tackle works to save their favorite lure. Jack Stafford buys out his partners within months, switches plating from nickel to silver, and grows production to tens of thousands of spoons a year.

1944 — 120 South Main Street

The company moves into the Tobey building, where the storefront and workshop remain today.

Today — Dierdre Stafford Stevenson

Jack's granddaughter runs the company with Debra Miles, forming spoons on the same presses — the third generation of Staffords behind the counter.

Naples Creek · Opening Day, Every April
Local Waters

Where the rainbows run, the derby registers at Sutton's.

Every spring the Naples Creek rainbow run brings anglers from across the Northeast, and for decades the opening-day Rainbow Trout Derby has run through this storefront — registration, weigh-in stories, and the spoon that wins it more often than not.

A 1950s tournament win on Lake Michigan with a Sutton 88 put the spoons on the national map. The reputation has traveled a long way from Academy Street — the spoons never had to.
